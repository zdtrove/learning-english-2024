A POLLUTED CANAL IN HO CHI MINH CITY

One environmental problem that can be seen in some parts of Ho Chi Minh City is polluted canals. The city has a large network of canals and waterways, and they play an important role in drainage and the daily lives of local communities. However, some canals have become seriously polluted over the years. When you look at these canals, you can immediately see that they are very different from what a clean and healthy waterway should look like.
In some polluted canals, the water is dark, muddy, and sometimes almost black. There may be plastic bags, bottles, food containers, pieces of foam, and other household waste floating on the surface. Some rubbish gets stuck along the banks, while other waste sinks to the bottom. The area around the canal may also look dirty because there is garbage scattered on the ground.
Another noticeable problem is the smell. On hot days, especially when there is little rain or water movement, polluted canals can produce a very unpleasant smell. The smell can spread to nearby houses, streets, and residential areas. For people who live close to the canal, this can become a daily problem rather than something they experience only occasionally.
There are several reasons why canals become polluted. One major reason is improper waste disposal. Unfortunately, some people still throw plastic bags, food waste, bottles, and other rubbish directly into canals. They may think that one small piece of rubbish does not make a difference, but when thousands of people behave in the same way over many years, the amount of waste becomes enormous.
Another important source of pollution is wastewater. Wastewater from households, restaurants, businesses, and other buildings can contain many different pollutants. If this wastewater is not properly collected and treated before entering waterways, it can gradually damage the quality of the water.
Rain can also make the situation worse. Ho Chi Minh City receives heavy rainfall during the rainy season. When it rains heavily, garbage on streets and in residential areas can be washed into drainage systems and eventually end up in canals. In this way, even waste that was not originally thrown directly into a canal can eventually become part of the pollution problem.
Canal pollution has serious effects on the environment. Dirty water can reduce the amount of oxygen available for aquatic life and make it difficult for fish and other organisms to survive. Some animals may become sick or die because of poor water quality. Plastic waste can also be dangerous because animals may eat it or become trapped in it.
Polluted canals can also affect the surrounding ecosystem. A healthy waterway is home to many different forms of life, but severe pollution can reduce biodiversity. When the water becomes too dirty, many plants and animals can no longer survive. Over time, the canal becomes less like a natural ecosystem and more like a drainage channel filled with waste.
The problem also affects local residents. People who live near a polluted canal may have to deal with unpleasant smells every day. During hot weather, the smell can become even stronger. Dirty water and accumulated garbage can also attract mosquitoes, flies, rats, and other pests. This can make people feel uncomfortable and worried about the cleanliness of their neighborhood.
There is also a connection between canal pollution and flooding. Canals and drainage systems are important for moving rainwater away from urban areas. When garbage accumulates in canals, drains, or nearby waterways, it can obstruct the flow of water. During heavy rain, this can contribute to drainage problems and make flooding worse in some areas.
Flooding is already a major challenge for Ho Chi Minh City because of heavy rainfall, rapid urban development, and the city's relatively low elevation in many areas. Therefore, keeping canals and drainage systems clear is important not only for environmental reasons but also for urban flood management.
Another consequence is the impact on the appearance of the city. Ho Chi Minh City is a large and rapidly developing city with many modern buildings, shopping centers, residential areas, and new urban projects. However, when a polluted canal runs through a neighborhood, it can make the entire area look less attractive.
This is particularly important because the quality of a city is not determined only by its buildings and roads. Public spaces, parks, rivers, canals, sidewalks, and residential neighborhoods also contribute to people's quality of life. A clean canal can become a pleasant part of a neighborhood, while a polluted canal can become an unpleasant and neglected area.
Solving canal pollution is not easy. In many cases, the pollution has accumulated over many years, so cleaning up the waterway once is not enough. If people continue to dump rubbish and untreated wastewater continues to enter the canal, the problem will simply return.
Another challenge is that pollution usually has many different sources. It is not always possible to identify one person, one household, or one business responsible for the entire problem. Effective solutions therefore require cooperation between the government, businesses, communities, and individual residents.
The government has an important role to play. First, it needs to improve wastewater collection and treatment systems so that polluted wastewater does not enter canals directly. Second, canals should be cleaned and maintained regularly. Garbage should be removed before it accumulates and blocks the flow of water.
The authorities can also strengthen environmental regulations and impose appropriate penalties on people or businesses that illegally dump waste into waterways. However, enforcement alone is not enough. The city should also invest in public education and explain why canal pollution is harmful to everyone.
Businesses also have a responsibility. Restaurants, factories, shops, and other businesses should manage their waste properly and make sure that wastewater is treated according to regulations. Companies should not simply focus on making money while ignoring the environmental impact of their activities.
Ordinary residents also have an important role. The simplest thing people can do is not throw rubbish into canals. They should put waste in appropriate bins and dispose of household waste properly. People can also reduce their use of single-use plastics and participate in community clean-up activities.
I believe changing people's habits is one of the most important parts of solving the problem. Infrastructure can be improved, canals can be cleaned, and new regulations can be introduced, but if people's behavior does not change, pollution will eventually come back.
At the same time, I don't think it is fair to blame ordinary people for everything. Environmental problems in a large city are complicated. The government needs to provide convenient waste-collection services, effective drainage systems, proper wastewater treatment, and enough public facilities for people to dispose of waste correctly.
In my opinion, the best solution is a combination of infrastructure, law enforcement, environmental education, and community participation. These four things need to work together. If only one of them is improved, the results may not last.
For example, cleaning a canal can immediately make it look better, but if rubbish continues to be thrown into the water, the canal may become polluted again within a short period of time. On the other hand, educating people without providing proper waste-management infrastructure may also have limited results.
I also think that polluted canals can be viewed as an opportunity to improve the city. Instead of seeing canals only as drainage channels, Ho Chi Minh City could gradually transform some suitable canal areas into cleaner and more attractive public spaces. The banks could potentially include walking paths, trees, small parks, or other community facilities where appropriate.
Of course, this would require a large amount of investment and careful urban planning. Not every canal can or should be transformed into a recreational area. Some waterways have important drainage functions and need to remain accessible for maintenance. However, where possible, improving the areas around canals could make neighborhoods more livable.
Ultimately, canal pollution is not just an environmental problem. It is also an urban planning problem, a public health concern, a waste-management issue, and a question of community responsibility.
A clean canal can improve the appearance of a neighborhood, reduce unpleasant smells, support aquatic life, improve drainage, and create a healthier living environment. A polluted canal, on the other hand, can affect the environment and people's everyday lives in many different ways.
Ho Chi Minh City is growing rapidly, and I think environmental protection needs to grow along with the city. Economic development and environmental protection should not be treated as completely separate goals. A modern city should not only have tall buildings, wide roads, and large shopping centers. It should also have clean waterways, healthy public spaces, efficient waste management, and a good quality of life for its residents.
In the end, protecting the city's canals is a shared responsibility. The government needs to provide the right infrastructure and enforce environmental regulations. Businesses need to manage their waste responsibly. And residents need to develop better habits and respect public spaces.
If all of these groups work together consistently, polluted canals can gradually become cleaner and healthier. It may take many years, but even small improvements can make a meaningful difference. A cleaner canal means a cleaner neighborhood, and cleaner neighborhoods can contribute to a healthier, more beautiful, and more livable Ho Chi Minh City.

MY STRENGTHS AND WEAKNESSES

If I had to describe my strengths and weaknesses, I would say that I am a responsible, patient, persistent, and relatively practical person. At the same time, I can sometimes overthink things, put too much pressure on myself, and be too critical of my own performance. I don't think anyone is perfect, including me, so I believe it's important to understand both our strengths and weaknesses if we want to grow as individuals. For me, knowing myself better is not about judging myself. It's about understanding how I think, how I behave, how I work with other people, and what I can do to become a better version of myself.
Over the years, I've become more aware of the kind of person I am. Of course, I don't understand myself perfectly, and I think our personalities can change as we gain more experience. However, there are certain characteristics that have remained quite consistent in my life. One of them is my sense of responsibility. When I accept a responsibility or make a commitment, I usually take it seriously. I don't like making promises that I don't intend to keep, and I generally try my best to finish what I've started.
This is particularly important to me at work. When I'm given a task, I don't simply want to finish it as quickly as possible. I want to understand what I'm doing and make sure that the final result is reliable. Of course, things don't always go according to plan. A task may turn out to be more difficult than expected, or an unexpected problem may appear. When that happens, I try not to simply give up. Instead, I try to communicate clearly, understand the situation, and find a practical solution.
I think being reliable is an important quality because people need to know that they can depend on you. Technical skills are important, especially in a professional environment, but skills alone aren't enough. If you're talented but unreliable, it can be difficult for other people to work with you. On the other hand, if people know that they can trust you to take your responsibilities seriously, that creates a strong foundation for teamwork.
However, I've also realized that responsibility can sometimes become a weakness if I take it too far. Sometimes I put too much pressure on myself because I feel that I should solve everything perfectly. I may feel uncomfortable when I make a mistake or when I can't control a situation. Over time, I've learned that being responsible doesn't mean trying to control everything. Sometimes being responsible also means asking for help, communicating problems early, accepting limitations, and understanding that some things are simply beyond my control.
Another important strength of mine is my willingness to learn. I enjoy learning things that are useful and meaningful, especially when I can understand how they work instead of simply memorizing information. This is particularly important because I work in IT, where technology changes very quickly. New frameworks, libraries, tools, and development practices are constantly appearing, so I don't think it's realistic to expect myself to know everything.
Instead, I think one of the most important skills for a developer is knowing how to learn. When I encounter a new technology, I usually start by understanding the basic concepts. Then I look at examples, try things myself, make mistakes, investigate problems, and gradually build a deeper understanding. I don't expect myself to understand everything immediately.
Sometimes something can seem extremely confusing at first. I may read the documentation or look at an example and still not completely understand what is happening. But if I spend enough time with it and try different approaches, eventually the pieces start to connect. Those moments are very satisfying because I know that I've actually understood something rather than simply memorized it.
I also consider adaptability one of my strengths. Life and work rarely stay exactly the same. Requirements change, projects change, technologies change, and unexpected situations appear. I don't always enjoy change, especially when it creates uncertainty, but I've learned that resisting every change isn't very useful.
Instead, I try to understand why the change is happening and figure out what I need to do differently. For example, if a project introduces a technology that I've never used before, my first reaction might be that it's going to be difficult. But after that initial reaction, I usually start thinking about how I can learn it and how I can break it down into smaller, more manageable parts.
I think adaptability is becoming increasingly important in modern life because we can't always predict what will happen in the future. A person doesn't necessarily have to know everything. They need to be able to learn, adapt, and continue moving forward even when circumstances change.
Problem-solving is another area where I feel relatively confident. When something goes wrong, I usually try not to panic. Instead, I prefer to break the problem down into smaller pieces. For example, when I encounter a technical bug, I don't immediately assume that the entire system is broken. I start by asking some basic questions. Where exactly does the problem happen? Can I reproduce it? What changed recently? Is the problem related to the frontend, backend, database, network, or configuration? What information do I actually have?
This way of thinking helps me move from a vague problem to a specific problem. Once the problem becomes more specific, it's usually easier to find a solution. I think this approach is useful not only in software development but also in everyday life. Many problems look complicated when we see them as one huge problem. But when we divide them into smaller parts, they often become much easier to handle.
Another strength I have is patience and persistence. I'm not someone who expects to become good at something overnight. If I want to improve a skill, I understand that it may take months or even years. This is especially true when learning a language or developing a professional skill.
At the beginning of a learning journey, it's easy to become frustrated because you may spend a lot of time practicing without seeing dramatic improvement. But if you continue practicing consistently, the improvement gradually becomes visible. I've learned that consistency is often more important than motivation.
Motivation can disappear. There are days when you feel tired, busy, or simply don't feel like doing something. But if you've developed a habit, you can continue even when your motivation is low. That's why I prefer making steady progress rather than trying to improve everything at once.
Self-discipline is closely related to this. For me, self-discipline means being able to do something even when I don't particularly feel like doing it. It's easy to work when you're highly motivated. The more difficult thing is continuing when you're tired or when the results aren't immediately visible.
I try to build routines around the things that are important to me. Instead of saying that I'll study whenever I have enough motivation, I prefer creating a regular learning habit. The same principle applies to exercise, work, and other personal goals. I don't think self-discipline means being extremely strict with yourself. It means creating a system that makes it easier to do the right thing consistently.
Another characteristic of mine is that I can be quite detail-oriented. When I'm working on something, I often notice small details that other people might ignore. This can be useful in software development because small details can sometimes cause big problems. A small mistake in a condition, configuration, user interface, or data flow can lead to unexpected behavior.
However, being detail-oriented also has a downside. Sometimes I spend too much time thinking about small details that don't have a significant impact on the final result. I've gradually learned that not every detail deserves the same amount of attention. Sometimes it's more important to finish the bigger picture first and then improve the smaller details later.
One of my biggest weaknesses is probably overthinking. When I have to make an important decision, I sometimes think about too many possibilities. I imagine what could go wrong, what might happen next, and whether there could be a better option.
The problem is that there is almost always another possibility. So if I keep thinking forever, I may never feel completely confident about my decision. I've realized that good decision-making doesn't mean having complete certainty. Sometimes you simply have to make the best decision you can based on the information available at that moment. Then, if new information appears, you can adjust your decision.
This is something I'm still working on. I'm trying to distinguish between useful thinking and unnecessary overthinking. Useful thinking helps me make a better decision. Overthinking, on the other hand, simply makes me feel more uncertain.
Another weakness is that I can sometimes be too self-critical. When I make a mistake, I tend to focus on the mistake itself. I may think about what I should have done differently and how I could have avoided the problem.
Of course, learning from mistakes is important. But I've learned that there is a difference between learning from a mistake and continuously criticizing yourself because of it. Making a mistake doesn't necessarily mean that I'm incompetent. Sometimes it simply means that I'm learning.
Instead of asking myself, "Why did I make this mistake?", I try to ask, "What can I learn from this?" That small change in perspective can make a big difference. It allows me to focus on improvement rather than simply feeling bad about what happened.
Communication is another area where I think I still have room for improvement. I'm generally comfortable communicating with people I know well, but spontaneous communication can sometimes be challenging.
For example, when I have to explain an unfamiliar idea immediately, I may need some time to organize my thoughts. Sometimes I know exactly what I want to say in my mind, but I don't express it as clearly as I would like.
This is one reason why I think speaking practice is so useful. Speaking isn't only about knowing vocabulary and grammar. It's also about organizing your thoughts quickly, choosing appropriate words, and expressing an idea naturally. I want to become someone who can explain complicated ideas in a simple and understandable way.
I think this is especially valuable for a developer. A developer doesn't only write code. We also need to explain technical issues, discuss solutions, understand requirements, communicate with team members, and sometimes explain complicated technical concepts to people who aren't technical.
At work, I would describe myself as cooperative and responsible. I don't think software development is something one person can do completely alone. Even if you're technically strong, you still need to communicate with designers, backend developers, testers, project managers, clients, and other team members.
I try to listen before giving my opinion. If someone has a different approach, I don't automatically assume that my approach is better. I think there are often several valid ways to solve a problem.
At the same time, I believe it's important to express your opinion when you have a good reason. Being cooperative doesn't mean agreeing with everyone. It means being able to discuss different opinions respectfully and focus on solving the problem rather than proving who is right.
Another important part of personal development is learning how to accept criticism. Nobody likes being told that they did something wrong. My natural reaction may sometimes be to explain why I did something a certain way. But I've learned that feedback can be extremely valuable.
Instead of immediately becoming defensive, I try to understand the other person's perspective. Maybe they noticed something I didn't notice. Maybe my solution works, but there is a better solution. Maybe I communicated something poorly.
The important thing is to separate criticism of my work from criticism of my identity. Someone saying that I made a mistake doesn't mean that I'm a bad developer or a bad person. It simply means there is something I can improve.
I also think failure is an important part of growth. Of course, nobody enjoys failing. But failure can teach us things that success sometimes doesn't. When everything goes well, we don't always think deeply about why it worked. But when something fails, we have to investigate. We have to ask what went wrong, what assumption was incorrect, and what we should do differently next time.
For me, the most important thing isn't avoiding every failure. It's avoiding the same failure repeatedly without learning from it. If I make a mistake once and learn something valuable from it, I can consider that experience useful.
When it comes to my emotional side, I think I'm relatively calm most of the time. I don't like reacting immediately when I'm angry or frustrated. I prefer taking some time to calm down and think about the situation.
However, being calm doesn't mean I never experience stress or negative emotions. Sometimes I worry about things that haven't happened yet. Sometimes I put too much pressure on myself. The important thing is learning how to manage those emotions instead of allowing them to control my behavior.
I think emotional maturity isn't about never feeling negative emotions. It's about understanding those emotions and responding to them appropriately.
Work-life balance is another area where I want to continue improving. When I become interested in something, I can become very focused on it. This is useful because focus helps me make progress. However, too much focus can also mean that I forget to relax.
I think personal development isn't only about becoming better at your job. It's also about having a healthy and enjoyable life. Spending time with family, exercising, having hobbies, relaxing, and simply doing nothing sometimes are also important.
Rest isn't necessarily a waste of time. Sometimes rest gives you the energy you need to work better later. I think a successful life isn't only about professional achievements. It's also about maintaining good relationships, enjoying your free time, taking care of yourself, and having a sense of balance.
One interesting thing I've realized is that strengths and weaknesses are sometimes two sides of the same characteristic.
For example, being responsible is a strength, but if I become too responsible, I may put too much pressure on myself. Being detail-oriented is useful, but if I focus too much on details, I may waste time. Being persistent is good, but if I refuse to change direction when necessary, persistence can become stubbornness. Thinking carefully is useful, but too much thinking becomes overthinking.
So I don't think the solution is simply to eliminate my weaknesses. Sometimes the better approach is learning how to control my strengths and use them appropriately.
I also don't have one magical method for improving myself. Instead, I believe in making small and consistent changes.
First, I try to become more aware of my behavior. If I notice that I'm overthinking, for example, I try to recognize it instead of automatically continuing. If I notice that I'm being too hard on myself, I try to step back and look at the situation more objectively.
Second, I try to learn from experience. After completing something important, I sometimes think about what went well and what could have been better. This kind of reflection helps me understand my own behavior and make better decisions in the future.
Third, I try to practice. If I want to improve communication, I need to communicate more. If I want to improve my English speaking, I need to speak more. If I want to become a better developer, I need to build things, solve problems, read documentation, and learn from other developers.
Knowledge is useful, but practice turns knowledge into ability.
When I think about the future, I don't have a goal of becoming a completely different person. I don't want to lose the qualities that already make me who I am.
Instead, I want to keep my sense of responsibility, patience, persistence, and willingness to learn while becoming more confident, flexible, communicative, and emotionally balanced.
I want to become someone who can stay calm when things go wrong, communicate clearly, make decisions without unnecessary hesitation, and accept mistakes without being too hard on myself.
I also want to become someone who can balance ambition with happiness. Being successful is important, but I don't think success should come at the cost of everything else in life. There is no point in achieving professional goals if you are constantly exhausted and have no time to enjoy your life.
Overall, I think knowing your strengths and weaknesses is an important part of growing as a person. My strengths help me achieve my goals, but my weaknesses remind me that I still have things to learn.
I don't expect myself to become perfect. Instead, I want to keep improving little by little. I believe personal growth is a long-term process. There will always be something new to learn, something to improve, and something to understand about yourself.
As I gain more experience, I think I will discover new strengths as well as new weaknesses. That's completely normal. We don't stay exactly the same throughout our lives.
For me, the most important thing is to keep moving forward. If I can become a little more knowledgeable, a little more confident, a little more patient, and a little more balanced every year, I think that's already meaningful progress.
In the end, I don't want to be the perfect version of myself. I simply want to become a better version of myself than I was yesterday. I think that's a much more realistic and meaningful goal, because personal growth isn't about becoming perfect. It's about continuing to learn, adapting to change, understanding yourself better, and becoming a little better over time.
