18 YEARS TRAPPED IN HANOI'S FLOOD-PRONE ALLEYS

For nearly 18 years, every time it rains, Cu Thi Bac switches to flood reflex mode, rushing to take her belongings up, including her bicycle, which she hangs on the wall.
On the morning of July 28, floodwaters in alley 42, Giang Van Minh, Ba Dinh Ward, receded partially following a 30-minute downpour the previous afternoon. At the alley entrance, six of the 40 houses were still under 20 centimeters of water.
Inside the house of Cu Thi Bac, 69, it was half a meter. "Because the house is 50 centimeters lower than the alley, it does not drain until the road is completely dry," Bac said.
Her family built the three-story house in 1993, and the flooding after a rain began in 2008 since the street was raised multiple times. She did not bother to raise her house after seeing that neighbors who did so still had water pouring in.
With three days of rain meaning seven days of floods, the family has moved all electrical sockets to two meters above the floor and installed a drainage pit at the gate and hangs bicycles on the wall. "Living in the middle of the capital is no different from living in a flood zone; worse, actually, because the whole house sits in foul-smelling sewage water," Bac lamented.
Daily life is upended if it rains. During less flooded days, she wades through the water to cook; on days when it is heavier, the whole family retreats to the second floor or eats at her younger sister's house nearby.
Bac's husband has moved back to their hometown to escape the waters. She suffers from joint disease, and so usually hires someone for VND250,000 (US$9.5) to dredge the mud after the waters recede.
The house of Nguyen Duc Minh, 38, at the intersection of alley 42 Giang Van Minh and sub-alley 34 of alley 100 Doi Can, is usually flooded from two directions.
With his rice meal business being run in a cramped space, it is difficult to take everything to higher ground when his home is inundated. Every time heavy rain is forecast, he and his wife move belongings up and park their motorbikes at a nearby lot.
After the July 27 downpour, the alley was under 60 centimeters of water, and Minh waded through thigh-deep water to deliver food to 50 customers.
With the water level staying high for more than a day, his wife Linh used a large laundry basin as a makeshift boat to row their child home from a kindergarten 300 meters away. "The road was deeply flooded and uneven, and so sitting the child in the basin and pulling them was safer," she said.
But the family cannot move because their livelihood depends on the meal business. After each flood, neighbors gather to help families with fewer able-bodied members dredge the mud and clean their homes.
As a defense against the routine flooding, 80% of people living there have raised their houses by 30-50 centimeters. Many families have also spent tens of millions of dong (VND10 million = $382)on installing metal flood barriers or automatic sensor systems in place of the old tarpaulin method.
A representative of the Hanoi Urban Infrastructure Management Center said at around noon on July 27, there was 50 mm of rain in 30 minutes, above the local drainage capacity.
Water rushed in rapidly, causing flooding of 35-40 centimeters in alleys 42 Giang Van Minh and 100 Doi Can. The center directed the Hanoi Drainage Company to stand by, clear debris, unblock drainage flows, and operate pumps.
According to Ngo Manh Son, head of residential group 15 in Ba Dinh Ward, the flooding in this area stems from a long-delayed project to culvert the Ke Khe canal and build a connecting road between Nui Truc and Son Tay.
The project for 1.04 kilometers of canal culverting and a 25-meter-wide road was approved by the city government in 2008 at a cost of VND205 billion.
The unfinished construction and littering habits that block drainage flows badly affect all residents in alleys 42 and 36 Giang Van Minh and alley 100 Doi Can. "Residential group 15 has repeatedly petitioned the authorities for a definitive solution, but the situation has not improved," Son said.
Businesses in the low-lying area suffer from financial losses. A café in alley 42 Giang Van Minh managed by Phan Duc Anh, 26, saw a sharp drop in sales every third quarter for three years.
Despite building the cafe 60 centimeters above the alley level, rains still submerge the furniture inside by 30-40 centimeters.
Hanoi has recently set a deadline of Dec. 31, 2026, for the completion of the Ke Khe canal culverting project. "I want to live in a dry house, with my belongings properly arranged to welcome guests, done with the years of piling everything up and running from floods," Bac said.

WHY VIETNAMESE YOUTH ARE MARRYING LATER THAN EVER

At 9:30 p.m., after the final order of the day is cleared, 35-year-old Bui Dung in Hanoi's Thanh Xuan Ward picks up his smartphone to begin his second daily job: finding a wife.
He types out a message to a 33-year-old woman recommended by a matchmaking service. He edited his words several times before hitting send, striving to hit the right tone—neither overly eager nor detached.
He then opens his inbox to respond to seven women who had responded to his post about looking for a wife on a match-making website. That eats up nearly two hours of his evening.
This routine has been standard for Dung for the last five years.
He has posted self-introductions on dating platforms six times, paid for professional matchmaking services, and even lost VND500 million (approx. US$20,000) to a scam targeting single men.
Now, he messages several women simultaneously to boost his odds.
Dung is far from alone in finding marriage elusive.
According to the General Statistics Office, Vietnam had some 20 million single adults at the end of 2024.
The average age at first marriage has climbed steadily. Between 1999 and 2019, it increased by 1.1 years to 25.2.
Over the following five years, the number jumped to 27.3, with several localities pushing past 30, a rise nearly eight times faster than in the previous two decades.
Sociologists attribute this trend to an "inflation" of life milestones. Young adults are delaying marriage as they spend longer on higher education, career stability, savings, and homeownership.
Prof. Dr. Ngo Thi Phuong Lan of the University of Social Sciences and Humanities in Ho Chi Minh city says intense competition and high living costs make balancing multiple major commitments difficult.
The transition to adulthood is taking longer, with financial stability and housing taking top priority.
"Marrying late isn't about young adults being overly picky," Lan says. "It is an active adaptation to the economic and social realities of modern living."
Dung's experience illustrates this shift. After working for a few years, he stepped in to help rescue his family's struggling lubricant business. By the time his job and income stabilized, he had passed 30.
With his social circle shrinking, he turned to matchmaking services.
"Matchmaking is tough because both sides are overly cautious, worried the other person isn't genuine, leaving you stuck in a loop," he says.
This reveals a paradox: the more time young people spend laying the groundwork for a stable marriage, the less time and space they have to actually find a partner.
"When someone hasn't fully established their own foundation, committing to a lifelong partnership becomes daunting," Lan says.
Thanh Ha, a 35-year-old civil servant in Khanh Hoa Province, faced a similar plight. She had once mapped out her trajectory: secure a stable job at 23, marry at 25, and have two children before 30.
Yet when she completed her master's degree at age 30, she realized finding a suitable partner was nearly impossible due to a limited dating pool.
Now, even after relaxing her criteria for physical appearance and financial standing, she remains single.
"At times, I feel completely adrift, terrified of spending the future alone," she admits.
The fundamental concept of marriage in Vietnam has transformed.
American sociologist Andrew Cherlin noted that in Vietnam, older generations typically wed in their early twenties with few assets, building their lives together and making marriage a "cornerstone," but now marriage is a "capstone", the final piece placed after all others are.
"It's not that marriage has lost its value, but rather its order in life's timeline has shifted," Lan says.
"Fewer people see marriage as a mandatory checkbox for a specific age."
Yet, meeting every benchmark offers no guarantee of finding a companion. Quang Minh, a 42-year-old construction company executive in Vinh Phuc Province, devoted his youth entirely to his career.
Once his company stood on solid ground, finding love proved agonizingly difficult.
His five-year relationship with a woman 14 years his junior ended because she wanted more time to experience her youth.
"Years ago, spending months away on construction sites made dating tough," Minh says.
"Now that I have financial stability and free time, romance is still just as hard."
Wei-Jun Jean Yeung, director of the Centre for Family and Population Research at the National University of Singapore, attributes Vietnam's delayed marriage trends to "compressed modernization," where social and economic changes unfold much faster than in western nations.
"Societal advances haven't aligned with traditional expectations placed on women to manage household duties," she tells VnExpress.
"When women become financially independent, marriage becomes optional, especially when the opportunity costs of becoming a wife and mother appear too high."
She notes that soaring housing costs and the rise of "intensive parenting" standards are giving rise to two distinct groups of young adults: those actively choosing singlehood and those priced out of marriage despite wanting it.
Lan acknowledges that the "capstone" model allows individuals to enter marriage with greater autonomy and personal maturity, but cautions against setting it in stone, pointing out that financial stability can easily be achieved jointly after marriage.
Addressing delayed marriage requires more than mere moral encouragement, according to Yeung.
"Policy must create an environment where young people can form families without sacrificing too much."
She says the solutions rest on five structural pillars: housing support, workplace gender equality, flexible work arrangements, long maternity leave, and good childcare networks.
Back in Hanoi, after sending his last email reply late at night, Dung switched off his device. Seeing the final bachelor in his friend group recently tie the knot has only heightened his impatience.
"I still dream of holding my child in my arms, and coming home after work to cook dinner and wash dishes with my wife," Dung said. "I just don't know when that dream will actually come true."

HCMC HOTEL TARIFFS SOAR BY 20% IN Q2

Average hotel room rates in HCMC rose by 20% year-on-year in the second quarter of the year to VND2.4 million (US$92) a night amid strong demand.
Average occupancy was over 70%, driven by demand from international visitors, businesses and Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions tourists, according to a recent report by property consultancy Savills.
Supply remained largely unchanged at around 17,000 rooms but for some marginal increases mainly due to the expansion of some three-star hotels. The market is seeing mostly upgrades rather than new developments, the report said.
Room rates rose even though the number of flights to the city fell by 4% due to higher fuel costs and airfares. In the first six months, the number of foreign visitors jumped by 50% year-on-year to 6.4 million, official data shows.
In the upscale segment, real estate consultancy Avison Young recorded average room rates of around VND5 million a night at five-star hotels, with occupancy consistently at 75%-80%.
Average rates at four-star hotels were around VND3.5 million, while occupancy was 72%-78%.
Demand for upscale accommodations remained stable from international visitors and business customers. The limited new supply meant existing hotels did not face much competitive pressure.
Property services firm JLL reported a 19.3% year-on-year increase in revenue per available room at HCMC hotels in the first quarter due to both higher tariffs and occupancy rates.
Savills said HCMC is not expected to see any major new hotel projects this year. By 2029, some 900 new four- and five-star rooms are expected to be added, almost all in the former District 1.
But supply will be unchanged in the short term, helping existing hotels maintain occupancy and room rates.
Analysts said, however, as customers increasingly prioritize brands and service quality, the limited supply might not benefit all hotels, and older properties, self-operated hotels, and those lacking investment could face greater pressure. These would need to renovate or reposition themselves to remain competitive, they added.
Mauro Gasparotti, senior director and head of hotel advisory for Southeast Asia at JLL, said: "The competition is increasingly being determined by quality, brand, and the ability to invest in upgrades."
Over the medium and long terms, the market is expected to attract more international brands. Avison Young said Caption by Hyatt in the Ba Son area is among the developments to watch.
In 2027-2028, brands including Nobu Hotel Ho Chi Minh City, Four Points by Sheraton and JW Marriott in Can Gio are expected to enter the upscale segment.
According to consulting firms, the hotel industry outlook remains positive, supported by growing international visitor numbers and the recovery of tourism across the Asia-Pacific.
HCMC hopes to attract 61 million visitors and generate about VND330 trillion in tourism revenues in 2026.

HANOI APARTMENT FLIPPERS TRAPPED AS MARKET DOWNTURN KEEPS BUYERS AWAY

Speculators who bought apartments in Hanoi from developers before or during construction are struggling to sell their units amid plunging prices and high mortgage interest rates.
As prices surged in recent years, many people bought apartments, hoping to flip them later. But then prices stopped rising and have even fallen in some areas, and these speculators are now looking to dump their properties quickly amid payment pressure.
Many early buyers were offered interest rate subsidies for a period, but now, with the grace period ending, many are facing high floating rates.
In late 2024, Thuy Vy, 35, bought a one-bedroom apartment in Gia Lam Commune in the city’s west for VND3 billion (US$114,300).
The property had just been launched when she bought, and she expected to sell for a profit when construction finished. It has been completed, and she has paid over half the price, and has to pay the remaining 45% next month.
For the last two months, she has been trying unsuccessfully to sell it at the same VND3 billion she paid. She has now reduced the price by VND150 million, but inquiries remain scarce.
She laments: "I regret not selling at the market peak. Liquidity is very low now."
In 2024, Tuan, 40, purchased an apartment in a nine-tower development bordering Thang Long Boulevard for about VND4 billion.
He has paid around half. With the developer scheduled to begin handing over units in October, he said, "I've never felt pressure like I do now."
He has to pay nearly VND2 billion, and bank interest rates have been rising steadily.
He is willing to sell for VND200 million below cost if someone wants to take it off his hands.
The two are among many who bought apartments in 2024-2025 and want to sell out now, but cannot find buyers due to a market slump.
In a recent market report, the Vietnam Association of Realtors said with many projects now entering the handover phase, buyers are required to pay the remaining 45%.
Historical listing data from online property platform Batdongsan shows that prices at several Hanoi localities and apartment projects have cooled from their peaks.
Asking prices have fallen by about 8% to 13%, including at Imperia Sola Park (down 12.6%), Lumi Hanoi (down 9.5%), and Kepler Land (down 8.6%).
Brokers say many speculators are abandoning short-term flipping strategies and merely hope to sell their properties as quickly as possible, reconciling to losses.
Duc Trung, a broker specializing in apartments in eastern Hanoi, said since the second quarter, the number of owners asking him to sell their apartments has surged by 20-30% compared with the beginning of the year.
Commenting on the trend, Pham Duc Toan, chief executive of real estate agency EZ Property, said many projects typically experience a wave of investors trying to exit their positions in the months leading up to handover, particularly those who relied heavily on leverage.
But he said selling apartments now is not easy, particularly at projects launched during the market boom in 2024.
With prices ranging from VND80 million to above VND100 million per square meter, secondary buyers must immediately commit several billion dong at a time when borrowing costs remain elevated, making them increasingly cautious.
Vo Huynh Tuan Kiet, director of the residential market at CBRE Vietnam, shared a similar view, saying apartment transactions in recent months have been driven primarily by speculators rather than people wanting to occupy them.
As prices move further beyond affordability, the market could enter a saturation phase in which "sellers are unwilling to lower prices, while buyers remain cautious about risks," he said.
He pointed to the 2007-2011 period, when Vietnam's housing market experienced a boom followed by tighter credit conditions, causing it to nearly freeze, and warned of similar risks emerging again.
Several research firms expect selling pressure from highly leveraged investors to intensify as huge supply enters the market.
From now through 2028, Cushman & Wakefield estimates more than 28,000 homes will be handed over in Hanoi.
The situation is worsened by high bank lending interest rates. Property agency Dat Xanh Services said mortgage rates are now 12-14%, and even 15-16% in many cases, and this has hit market liquidity.
It estimates the absorption rate at only 20-30% of supply in the first six months of the year, down from 50-60% in the second half of 2025.
Toan of EZ Property said most investors currently facing financial distress had relied on excessive leverage and should consider restructuring their mortgages and extend repayment rather than resorting to distressed sales.

WHY MORE VIETNAMESE SENIORS ARE CHOOSING APARTMENTS OVER PRIVATE HOMES

Many older Vietnamese who once resisted leaving their private houses are now choosing to live in apartments for greater safety, convenience, and tightening family bonds.
At 71, Tran Thi Van Anh never imagined she would leave the house she had lived in since 1990 on Thai Ha Street in Hanoi despite its poor natural lighting and constant traffic noise. She occupied one floor and her son Nam Trung, 44, lived with his wife and two children on another.
Despite three generations living under one roof, their daily lives did not intersect. "We rarely talked to each other, and I was always worried about leaving my mother home alone," Trung says.
He decided to move the whole family into an apartment, a plan Van Anh initially opposed due to fears of crowded communal spaces and long elevator queues. But within two weeks of moving into their new home, she changed her mind.
The home is a 180-square-meter dual-key apartment situated on the eighth floor of a tower in Lang Ha Street. The layout provides two independent living spaces with a shared main entrance.
Van Anh occupies the section with a southeast-facing bedroom and large balcony adjacent to the family altar and shares the living room.
Her son's family has their own separate bedroom and working area. Without the need to climb stairs and with 24-hour security, Van Anh sleeps much better. Having the family on a single level also means she spends more time with her grandchildren throughout the day. "When guests come over, she's now the one proudly showing them around, especially the sunny balcony," Trung says.
Tran Van Khang, 78, used to live in a two-story garden home in Gia Lam District, Hanoi, with his wife, Le Thi Hoa, 75, fully intending to spend the rest of his life there. As he grew older, the property became larger than he needed, turning into what he describes as "a lonely nest."
"My daughters were both busy. Even though they lived only about 20 kilometers away, they managed to visit just a few times a year."
But the key motivation for moving came when Hoa began to help care for their eldest daughter’s children, meaning she needed to live nearby. Both daughters contributed financially to help their parents move into an apartment.
Today, Khang and Hoa live in an 80-square-meter sixth floor apartment in Xuan Dinh Ward, while one of their daughters lives on the 20th. Khang no longer worries about falling on the stairs.
He spends his days cooking, playing with his grandchildren and meeting fellow retirees in the neighborhood for games of chess.
Stories like those of Van Anh and Khang reflect a broader global shift toward age-friendly housing that fosters multi-generational co-living amid rapid aging in Vietnam. For many families, transitioning to single-level living spaces is a way to safeguard seniors from risks.
According to property platform Batdongsan.com.vn, apartments have been the preferred housing choice of 59% of homebuyers in Hanoi and 54% in HCMC in the first half of 2026.
Matthew Powell, director of Savills Hanoi, believes the trend is driven by three long-term structural changes. First, Vietnam is experiencing rapid, irreversible population aging before reaching high-income status, creating strong demand for housing that integrates safety and care rather than simply providing shelter.
Second, rapid urbanization and the dual pressure of work and childcare have made it increasingly difficult for families to sustain the traditional model of caring for aging parents at home. While around 80% of older Vietnamese wish to age at home, existing care services meet only a small fraction of that demand.
Third, senior housing has already become a mature multi-hundred-billion-dollar asset class globally, integrating real estate with healthcare, services, and social welfare.
Dr. Ngo Viet Nam Son, an architect with over three decades of international experience, says senior housing in Japan, Singapore, Australia, and Europe varies in style but consistently integrates clinics, rehabilitation centers, and parks.
Real estate analyst Le Quoc Kien believes the demographic shift is already influencing housing design in Vietnam.
Demand for multi-bedroom units, split-level duplexes, and dual-key layouts is set to grow to accommodate multi-generational families living together or side by side, he says.
Hai Van, a 72-year-old retiree previously working in aviation, has experienced those benefits firsthand. Family gatherings used to require her children to drive across the city to pick her up, with trips at times canceled due to rain.
To change this, Van's eldest daughter initiated a move, pooling capital with her sister and mother to purchase units in the same compound.
Today, the family lives in three separate towers within the same residential complex in Hanoi's Nghia Do Ward, where they simply take the elevator down to the interconnected basement parking lot to meet.
Since moving into her 75-square-meter apartment, Van regularly joins community dance classes, yoga sessions, and swimming activities, seeing her children and grandchildren three to five times a week. "At this point, I fully support older people moving into apartments," she says.
From an eldercare perspective, Tran Thuy Nga, deputy general director of Dien Hong Nursing Home, says many people still romanticize the idea of ageing in detached houses with gardens.
In reality, many retirees gradually limit themselves to just a few rooms, leading to sedentary lifestyles and increased fall risks. According to the World Health Organization, roughly 30% of people aged 65 and over, and nearly half of those aged over 80, experience at least one fall a year, with half suffering recurrent falls.
Nga points out: "Even healthy older adults can slip on steps or stairs. A single fall can cost someone their independence."
She also notes that seniors not only need a flat living space but an environment that encourages them to step outside, meet neighbors, join clubs, and feel a sense of belonging. "A well-designed apartment community does not just help older people live safely; it must also help them avoid living in isolation."
A 2026 study of 1,000 older adults in China found that flat housing with elevators helps foster seniors’ independence. The ability to stroll, shop, and socialize unassisted yields profound benefits for mental health.
To prepare for this demographic shift, Son believes Vietnam needs to rethink apartment design, moving beyond housing primarily intended for young families to inclusive, age-friendly environments.
Safety features such as non-slip flooring, handrails, and emergency alert systems should become standard rather than optional, he says.
For Van Anh, the meaning of a fulfilling old age has also changed. "If a garden and courtyard once symbolized settling down, today what truly matters is having a single-level home where I can open the door and see my children and grandchildren, or take the elevator downstairs to meet friends."
