GRAB DRIVERS CALL FOR STRIKE, CITING FALLING INCOMES

Citing low fares and high commission rates, many Grab drivers are urging their colleagues to log off the platform on Sept. 12-13 to pressure the company to revise its policies.

Xuan Hung, 28, has worked as a ride-hailing driver in Hanoi since 2020. Since switching to driving a car in late 2024, he has been working 14-15 hours a day to earn around VND30 million (about $1,150) a month.

When he first started driving, he could earn tens of millions of dong a month while working only part time. Grab currently pays drivers just VND4,000-5,000 per kilometer for car trips and VND2,500 for motorbike trips, compared with VND6,000-7,000 on rival platforms, Hung said. "Fares are so low that I have to turn off the Grab app and drive for other companies instead."

Many of his colleagues are also dissatisfied because total deductions can reach 30-50%, depending on when they drive, while drivers have to cover fuel and vehicle wear and tear themselves, he added.

Duc Anh, 35, of Hoang Liet Ward in Hanoi, said he completed a 10-kilometer trip two days earlier for which the app displayed a fare of VND50,000. After commissions and other fees were deducted, he received just over VND30,000. "After covering my operating expenses, what I take home is not enough to cover my living costs."

On an online forum for ride-hailing drivers with more than 312,000 members, many Grab drivers said they earned less than VND150,000 after five hours on the road. Some reported taking home just VND11,000-12,000 for trips of four to five kilometers.

A car driver in Ho Chi Minh City said he received only VND26,000 for a four-kilometer trip. Another in Hai Phong said he received VND157,000 for a trip that cost the passenger VND262,000, with the difference going toward fees and taxes.

These individual trips were not representative of all activity on the platform, a VnExpress review found.

Nevertheless, such anecdotes have prompted drivers in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City to call for a collective "turning off of the app" on Sept. 12-13 to pressure the company to reconsider its revenue-sharing rates.

Grab charges motorbike and car drivers commissions of 20% and 25%, respectively. A driver's payout is calculated after taxes and platform fees are also deducted. Operations remain normal, a Grab spokesperson told VnExpress on the evening of Sept. 10.

The company is aware of discussions among drivers on social media and is carefully assessing the situation, but it has always prioritized drivers' livelihoods and service quality, the person added.

Changes to commission rates and bonus policies have prompted Grab drivers to collectively log off on several previous occasions.

In 2017, the company raised its app-use fee from 5% to 20% in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, saying the change was intended to standardize its policies and improve competitiveness.

Many drivers responded by logging off simultaneously and driving through city streets in protest. In January 2018, drivers staged large protests in northern and southern Vietnam after the company raised its commission rate for car and motorbike services from 20% to 23.6%. It subsequently restored the rate to 20%.

In mid-2019, numerous GrabBike drivers gathered at the company's Ho Chi Minh City office to protest higher fees resulting from taxes collected on their behalf.

The company stopped withholding personal income tax from its drivers soon afterward. In December 2020, hundreds of drivers gathered in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City to protest tax deductions and an increase in service fees from 20% to more than 27.2%.

To ease tensions while offsetting the additional costs, Grab raised fares. In 2023 and 2024, complaints surfaced periodically in major cities, with drivers saying the trip-assignment algorithm had changed, bonus policies were inconsistent, and account suspensions lacked transparency.

Drivers for Grab and several rival platforms periodically stopped accepting trips.

Ride-hailing drivers' incomes have recently declined because platforms have lowered fares to attract and retain customers while leaving commission rates unchanged, Le Tan Luu, chairman of the Binh Tan App-Based Motorbike Taxi Drivers Union in Ho Chi Minh City, said.

Actual earnings also depend on bonuses, he explained. Drivers who meet trip targets within specified periods can offset lower fares with bonuses, while those who work part time are less able to do so, he said.

But he suggested that drivers should not act in haste. "Drivers should avoid spontaneous gatherings that could disrupt traffic, public order or security. They should also avoid hastily turning off the app."

He urged drivers instead to compile data on trips they considered underpriced and appoint representatives to meet directly with Grab and regulators to resolve the issue.

Despite the calls for a stoppage, some drivers remained on the road. Duc Minh, 45, of Hanoi, said he was still picking up passengers but accepted only trips offering better fares. "If I turn off the app now, my family will lose income."


MORE THAN A DECADE ON, VIETNAM'S 600,000 GIG DRIVERS REMAIN UNINSURED WORKERS

More than 600,000 ride-hailing and delivery drivers in Vietnam remain classified as business partners rather than employees, leaving them without insurance or injury coverage even as other markets move to guarantee such protection.

More than a decade after Grab and Uber brought the gig economy to Vietnam, the country's roughly 600,000 ride-hailing and delivery drivers still have no legal status as employees. The "partner" label that platforms have used since 2014 spares companies from paying social insurance, health insurance or unemployment insurance, while Vietnamese law has yet to determine whether these drivers are entitled to any of it. Meanwhile, Britain, France, the European Union and neighboring Singapore have all moved to bring their own platform workers under legal protection.

When the model first arrived, almost no one questioned it. The pitch was the "sharing economy," in which an app merely connected someone who needed a ride with someone who owned an idle vehicle, with the two sharing the cost of fuel.

Huynh Long, then 32 and a factory worker at the Tan Thuan Export Processing Zone in Ho Chi Minh City, signed up to drive for GrabBike to pay off a motorbike loan.

"In the early days, the bonuses were worth more than what passengers paid," he recalled. Within a year, his app income had matched his factory wage, and he quit to drive full time, alongside co-workers hoping to earn VND15-20 million ($570-760) a month.

"The positives at the time drowned out almost the entire debate over labor relations," said Nguyen Tat Nam, former head of the labor and social insurance division at the Ho Chi Minh City Department of Labor, Invalids and Social Affairs, now the Department of Home Affairs.

The market grew from about $200 million in 2015 to $1.6 billion in 2020, according to a Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung report on Vietnam's digital economy, while the workforce swelled into the hundreds of thousands, with around 600,000 drivers in the 2014-2019 period alone. Covid-19 accelerated the shift as workers laid off elsewhere turned to driving.

But as the numbers climbed, earnings fell. A 2021 survey by the Vietnam General Confederation of Labor, the Center for Health Consultancy and Community Development, and Oxfam found that motorbike drivers took home just VND7-9 million ($266-342) a month after expenses, often working 8 to 13 hours a day. Nearly 23% regularly drove between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., exposing themselves to accidents, robbery and harassment without any of the coverage an employer would be required to provide.

Vietnamese courts have already signaled that the "partner" framing does not hold. In a lawsuit between Grab and Vietnam's taxi giant Vinasun, which was settled between 2018 and 2020, judges found that Grab operated not as a neutral intermediary but as a transport company that set fares, dispatched rides and managed its drivers, even cutting their pay over customer complaints. The 2019 Labor Code went further, establishing that an arrangement can qualify as employment, whatever it is called, when it involves paid work performed under management, direction and supervision.

The problem, said Dinh Thi Chien, a lecturer at the Ho Chi Minh City University of Law, is that the law never defined how much control crosses that line, and platform control does not resemble the traditional kind. Drivers answer to no manager, but to an algorithm that distributes rides, scores them automatically and can quietly end a career through acceptance rates, cancellations and customer ratings.

"If you look only at the name of the contract, it is very hard to identify an employment relationship," she said. "The point is to look at the substance of that relationship." That substance, mediated through data, has opened a legal gray zone that the rules have yet to catch up with.

Other markets stopped arguing over the label and started assigning responsibility, often based on reasoning strikingly similar to that of Vietnam's own courts. Britain's Supreme Court ruled in 2021 that Uber drivers are workers entitled to the minimum wage and paid holiday. France's highest court reclassified an Uber driver as an employee in 2020, finding his "independent" status fictitious because the platform set fares, tracked his performance and could discipline him. The European Union adopted a directive in 2024 presuming platform workers are employees unless companies prove otherwise. Singapore went furthest, with a dedicated law taking effect Jan. 1, 2025, requiring platforms to contribute to drivers' retirement savings and injury insurance.

The International Labor Organization, in its report "Protecting Workers in New Forms of Employment," argues that the question is no longer whether gig drivers are workers, but how to determine the true nature of their relationship with the platform, weighing the reality of the work and the degree of control rather than the wording of the contract. It urges governments to extend social protection to platform workers even when they are classified as self-employed.

More than a decade on, Chien said, Vietnam's gig drivers are no longer a novelty of the digital economy, and what is missing is not debate but a framework clear enough to protect them.

"The question now is not whether to regulate, but how to regulate so that they do not slip through the safety net simply because they work through an app," she said.


HOLIDAY "BED ROTTING" LEAVES YOUNG PEOPLE MORE EXHAUSTED THAN REFRESHED

Many young people spent Vietnam’s five-day National Day holiday lying in bed in hopes of recovering from work stress, only to return feeling even more exhausted.

Pham Ha, 25, an office worker in Hanoi’s Cau Giay area, barely left her home during the break. She stayed up scrolling through social media until 3 a.m., slept until 2 p.m., ate just one meal a day, and then went back to bed.

Before the holiday began on Aug. 29, she deleted her work messaging apps and silenced her phone.

“Weeks of working late into the night to meet back-to-back deadlines had worn me out. Once the holiday came, all I wanted was to lie still, see no one, and think about nothing,” she says.

By the final day, however, she was aching all over and suffering from headaches and dizziness. Instead of feeling refreshed for the week ahead, she felt even worse.

Minh Thu, 27, a communications worker in Hanoi, also spent most of the holiday in bed at her parents’ house in central Nghe An Province.

Just fighting the crowds to get a ticket and traveling more than 300 kilometers home had left her exhausted. So she canceled plans to go out with friends and family, confined herself to her room, where she ate in bed, watched movies, listened to music, and slept.

“By the time I returned to the city for work, my mind was blank. I felt more sluggish and tired than before,” she says.

Ha and Thu are among a growing number of young people embracing “bed rotting,” a term that originated on TikTok to describe spending days on end in bed to escape work pressures and social interactions.

In a VnExpress survey of nearly 1,000 readers, 34% said they regularly engaged in bed rotting on days off. U.S. research firm Morning Consult found that 49% of Gen Z did so regularly or occasionally, compared with 41% of millennials. Among Gen Z, 62% had canceled social plans just to stay in bed, while 53% said they felt guilty or empty afterward.

Dr. Nguyen Viet Chung, head of the mental health department at Hanoi’s E Hospital, says the behavior has clear psychological roots in pressure to perform, meet KPIs, and live up to social expectations, which can keep young people in a constant state of stress.

A 2023 UNICEF report said that 20% of adolescents in Vietnam faced mental health issues, while a NielsenIQ survey found that 65% of young people felt pressured to outperform their peers.

Against this backdrop, Chung says sleeping in, reading, and unplugging from work for a single weekend day could help the body recover. But when the inactivity stretches into a third or fourth day, the line between recharging and avoiding reality begins to disappear.

“At a certain point, staying in bed is no longer rest. When people begin to lose motivation and feel guilty but still cannot get themselves off the mattress, the behavior has become an unhealthy coping mechanism. It may even be an early sign of anxiety or depression.”

He lists three physical effects of staying in bed for prolonged periods:

**Disrupted sleep-wake cycle:** Sleeping out of sync with the body’s natural day-night rhythm disrupts hormone release, leaving people drowsy during the day and unable to sleep at night.

**Poorer circulation:** Inactivity slows metabolism. After several days, muscles and joints stiffen, leaving people feeling even more fatigued.

**Constant stimulation of the cerebral cortex:** Using a phone in bed prevents the brain from fully resting and weakens the association between bed and sleep, potentially leading to sleep disorders.

“People need to know the difference between allowing themselves to rest and no longer having enough energy to function. The two may look similar, but they are fundamentally different.”

To make time off truly restorative, he recommends keeping a consistent wake time, sleeping seven to eight hours a night, and avoiding long daytime naps to make up for lost sleep. He also advises reserving the bed for sleep and taking activities such as reading and phone scrolling elsewhere.

Rather than staying glued to a screen, young people should interrupt their scrolling with a short activity every two hours, spend 15-30 minutes in sunlight each day, and do some light exercise, he says.

After finding in the past that oversleeping left him exhausted, Tuan Minh, 24, of HCMC, decided to structure his days off differently this year.

He woke at 7 a.m., ran for 30 minutes, cooked three meals a day, and spent time reading.

“Work keeps me busy and makes me eat at irregular hours. I used the holiday to get back to a healthier routine,” he says.


WHY VIETNAM IS BRINGING SATELLITE INTERNET TO CONNECTIVITY BLACKSPOTS

Vietnam has begun rolling out satellite Internet to provide reliable connectivity in cellular dead zones across 13 provinces and cities.

Across the country, 273 remote villages and hamlets currently lack reliable mobile reception or remain entirely cut off from cellular networks, including 82 communities that are not connected to the national power grid.

Another 44 locations, mostly rural schools, health clinics, and isolated areas surrounded by rugged terrain, struggle with weak or intermittent reception.

In a September report, the National Steering Committee on Science, Technology, Innovation, and Digital Transformation directed relevant agencies to ensure network coverage for these priority locations by Oct. 30.

Difficult terrain, scattered populations, and a lack of power grids make these sites difficult to reach and economically unviable for conventional ground-based infrastructure, Le Xuan Thang, an infrastructure development official at the Authority of Telecommunications, explained.

"A single solution cannot resolve every dead zone. That is why satellite Internet serves as a vital supplementary measure."

In Son La, a northern mountainous province where more than 80% of the land is rugged terrain and travel remains arduous, telecom carriers have worked hard to expand cellular networks, but several pockets "still need more time," Pham Quoc Chinh, deputy director of the provincial Department of Science and Technology, said.

"The province currently faces coverage hurdles across 53 locations, including 36 villages and hamlets in exceptionally disadvantaged areas."

Because building terrestrial networks in these areas requires significant time and capital while local demand for connectivity is urgent, Son La received 36 Starlink satellite terminals along with six months of free service.

Chinh emphasized that satellite access has "profound significance" for the province, giving isolated communities an opportunity to "gain early access to online services."

The satellite terminals are designated strictly for public use rather than private households. They are installed at communal hubs where local authorities can monitor network performance, user traffic, and the number of actual beneficiaries. Equipment safety has also been factored into the plan: if a village cultural hall lacks adequate security, authorities will move the installation to a monitored facility with dedicated oversight.

Eliminating coverage blind spots requires ongoing updates because infrastructure expansion, population shifts, and changing connectivity demands could create new dead zones over time, Thang said.

For areas that remain out of reach in the short term, satellite connectivity serves as an immediate stopgap.

Starlink is a satellite broadband constellation developed by Elon Musk's SpaceX that connects user terminals to low Earth orbit satellites, which route data through ground stations to provide access to the global web.

It was the first foreign company to receive regulatory clearance to pilot satellite Internet in Vietnam and launched commercial operations in August.

The equipment donation fulfills a commitment made by SpaceX and Starlink Services Vietnam during their licensing bid to supply 1,000 Starlink kits and six months of free data service to support schools, clinics, rural communities, and underserved groups.


# WHY SOME VIETNAMESE WORKERS ARE AFRAID TO LEAVE THE OFFICE ON TIME

At nearly 7 p.m., Thuc Uyen and her colleagues were still at their desks, staring at their laptops despite having little work left to do. None of them wanted to be the first to leave before their boss.

Official working hours at Uyen’s private company in HCMC end at 5:30 p.m., but the 32-year-old marketing employee says she now routinely stays until after 7:30 p.m. or waits for her manager to leave first.

She used to go home on time until a performance review two years ago, when she received an A- rating and a bonus that was US$192 lower than those of several colleagues.

Her department head told her points had been deducted for her "work attitude" because she did not stay late like others and was therefore seen as less enthusiastic. "I don’t know when leaving work on time started being seen as a lack of dedication," Uyen says.

Van Vy, 29, an IT employee in Hanoi, had a similar experience after failing to secure a salary increase in 2025.

Although his job performance was good, his supervisor said he did not appear to be putting in enough effort because he "stood up to leave at 5:30 p.m. every single day."

Vy has since adjusted his routine, spending more time over coffee in the morning and deliberately pushing work into the afternoon so he can remain at the office until around 7 p.m.

The issue has become a frequent topic of debate on social media.

One video discussing employees who leave as soon as working hours end drew 4.3 million views, while another debate over whether leaving on time reflects low emotional intelligence attracted nearly 270,000 viewers.

Hundreds of posts have also appeared on Threads under the theme of being unable to leave work on time.

Opinions are divided. Some argue that leaving on schedule after completing assigned tasks is professional, while others see an unwillingness to stay late as a sign of weak commitment.

A VnExpress survey in August 2026 found that 13% of businesses assessed employees partly based on the amount of time they spent in the office. Another poll of 2,000 readers found that 13% of workers finished their tasks on time but stayed behind because they feared being judged.

This pressure to remain visible at work, even when productivity has dropped, is often described as presenteeism.

A survey by U.K.-based health and wellbeing company Vitality found that presenteeism reduced productivity far more than absenteeism, costing an estimated 35 working days per employee each year.

Still, some managers defend the practice.

Thanh Toan, 45, director of an import-export company in Hai Phong, prefers employees to stay late because their physical presence can make coordination between departments easier and allow problems to be handled more quickly.

He also believes voluntarily remaining after hours can signal responsibility, commitment and a willingness to put collective goals ahead of personal interests.

Dr. Le Trung Hieu, deputy head of the human resource management department at the School of Business Administration, Vietnam National University, Hanoi, says Vietnam’s workplace culture has been shaped partly by its economic history and by increasing integration with East Asian business practices.

"Foreign investment from East Asian countries have brought with it a work environment that emphasizes hierarchy, obedience to superiors, and consensus, along with fierce competitive pressure."

Hieu adds that the reluctance to leave on time is also rooted in a widespread workplace assumption that longer hours reflect greater dedication.

When companies lack clear performance indicators, managers may fall back on time spent at the office as a proxy for commitment, he says.

From a psychological perspective, experts say some managers prefer to keep employees physically visible because it gives them a greater sense of control.

Employees, meanwhile, may avoid leaving early because they fear conflict, being seen as less capable or losing promotion opportunities.

Nguyen Thi Kieu Chinh, a financial adviser at FIDT Investment Consulting and Asset Management Joint Stock Company, says the phenomenon is particularly common in finance, technology and consulting in major cities, where daily output can be difficult to quantify.

"Many people accept staying late because they fear appearing unmotivated or missing out on promotion opportunities," she says.

But she warns that this kind of superficial display of commitment only offers short-term reassurance. Over time, it can erode motivation, increase staff turnover and drive away capable employees.

It can also damage health and work-life balance. "A competitive economy is not built on the number of hours worked, but on the value generated during each hour," Chinh says.

In Hanoi, sales manager Minh Phong, who oversees 20 employees, says he stopped judging staff by how late they stayed after realizing it did not improve performance. "I manage by deadlines for task completion, not by watching over adults like a preschool teacher," he says.

Hieu recommends that businesses shift toward output-based management, establish transparent KPI systems and require employees to communicate progress clearly.

He says workplace cultures built around deference will not change overnight, but small changes within individual teams can gradually spread. "Changes in microculture will gradually reshape the professional work environment," he says.

Minh Phuong, 35, an accountant in HCMC, says she now asks about working hours and overtime expectations during job interviews and only stays late when genuinely urgent work arises.

"Leaving on time when the job is done is a sign of professionalism," she says.
